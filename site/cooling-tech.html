Thermal Engineering Analysis

Thermodynamic mechanics of closed-loop cooling

Cooling historically consumed up to 40% of a data center's electricity. As rack densities pass 50 to 100 kW, air cooling and evaporative towers hit physical limits — and the industry is switching to sealed liquid loops that use no water at all.

GreenCompute Research · Updated September 3, 2026

The water consumption baseline

Facility efficiency is quantified with two standardized metrics, defined by ASHRAE Technical Committee 9.9 and The Green Grid:[1]

PUE =
Total Facility Energy Consumption (kWh) IT Equipment Energy Consumption (kWh)
WUE =
Annual Water Consumption (Liters) IT Equipment Energy Consumption (kWh)
Standardized by ASHRAE Technical Committee 9.9 & The Green Grid

Traditional data centers rely on open-loop evaporative cooling towers. Evaporation dissipates heat via the latent heat of vaporization, consuming 1.5 to 3.0 liters per kWh and yielding an industry-average WUE of 1.8 L/kWh. Under peak summer loads, a large hyperscale campus running open-loop towers consumes 1 to 5 million gallons of potable water daily — an extraction rate comparable to a city of 30,000 to 50,000 residents.[1]

About 70 to 80% of that water is lost to the atmosphere through evaporation; the remaining 20 to 30% is discharged as mineral-dense blowdown wastewater. Off-site thermoelectric and hydroelectric generation adds another 7.6 liters per kWh across the utility supply chain.[1]

Closed-loop direct-to-chip (DLC) mechanics

To eliminate on-site water consumption, modern architectures deploy closed-loop Direct-to-Chip liquid cooling paired with exterior closed-circuit dry coolers:

  1. Primary loop: a sealed loop circulates treated dielectric fluid or water through micro-channel cold plates mounted directly against GPUs and CPUs. Heat conducts from silicon into the fluid.
  2. Technology Cooling System (TCS): the hot primary fluid passes through a TCS heat exchanger, transferring thermal energy to an isolated secondary loop.
  3. External dry coolers: the secondary loop pumps rejected heat to rooftop or exterior dry coolers with high-efficiency variable-speed fan banks, shedding heat into ambient air with no fluid contact with the atmosphere.

Once charged during commissioning, the closed system operates with zero evaporative water consumption — a verified operational WUE of 0.0 L/kWh. The flagship deployments are profiled on the facilities page: Microsoft's 172-fan DLC campuses, NVIDIA's sealed 45°C propylene-glycol DSX loop, and Meta's dry-cooling coils.[2]

"Once charged with fluid during initial construction, the closed system operates with zero evaporative water consumption, saving over 125 million liters of water per facility annually compared to evaporative towers." — GreenCompute synthesis of operator disclosures

The warm-climate trade-off

Closed loops shift the heat-rejection burden to ambient air, and in warm climates that means more fan power — a slight PUE increase. Operators compensate by raising server inlet fluid temperatures above 27°C, expanding the hours of passive economization and keeping water at zero without an energy penalty.[1]

This is exactly the lever Germany's EnEfG § 11(6) now mandates: air-cooled facilities must maintain inlet temperatures of at least 27°C to make waste heat commercially viable for district heating. The statutory side of the story is on the regulation page.
