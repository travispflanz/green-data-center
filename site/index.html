How AI rewired the data center: power, water, and the law

The rise of artificial intelligence turned data centers from commercial real estate into utility-scale electrical and thermal infrastructure. This is the story of that transformation — and the engineering and law being built to contain it.

GreenCompute Research · Updated September 3, 2026

The density problem

Conventional enterprise data centers ran at 5 to 10 kW per rack, drawing predictable loads that utilities handled through standard medium-voltage feeds. AI training clusters changed that: dense arrays of GPUs and TPUs push racks to 50 to 200 kW, and by 2030 AI-optimized hardware is projected to account for 44% of global data center power consumption.[1]

The International Energy Agency projects total data center electricity use will exceed 945 TWh by 2030 — roughly Japan's entire annual demand. In the United States, data center electricity grew from 76 TWh in 2018 to 176 TWh in 2023 (4.4% of national demand) and is forecast to reach 325 to 580 TWh by 2028.[1]

AI workloads are also violently volatile. Training clusters draw sustained peaks, then drop hundreds of megawatts in fractions of a second during checkpointing, then surge back. Inference adds sharp, high-frequency spikes that can account for up to 90% of a model's lifecycle energy. Utilities now see hyperscale campuses as grid-scale batteries with compute attached — and hyperscalers respond with utility-scale battery storage behind the meter.[1]

The water problem

Heat is the second constraint. Cooling historically consumed up to 40% of a data center's electricity, and the dominant legacy technology — open-loop evaporative cooling towers — consumes 1.5 to 3.0 liters of water per kilowatt-hour. A large hyperscale campus running cooling towers can draw 1 to 5 million gallons of potable water a day, an extraction rate comparable to a city of 30,000 to 50,000 residents.[2]

Roughly 70 to 80% of that water is lost to evaporation; the rest is discharged as mineral-dense blowdown. Add the supply chain and the number grows: off-site thermoelectric and hydroelectric generation consumes another 7.6 liters per kWh across the grid.[2]

Public scrutiny of industrial water withdrawals is now a siting constraint as real as grid access. That pressure is driving the industry's most consequential mechanical shift: closed-loop, zero-water cooling.

The closed-loop answer

Direct-to-chip (DLC) liquid cooling mounts micro-channel cold plates directly on the silicon, circulates treated dielectric fluid or water through a sealed primary loop, and rejects heat to exterior dry coolers — no evaporation, no water contact with the atmosphere. Once charged at commissioning, the system runs at a verified WUE of 0.0 L/kWh, saving over 125 million liters of water per facility annually.[2]

Microsoft's next-generation AI campuses in Phoenix and Mount Pleasant, Wisconsin, are the flagship example: factory-sealed DLC loops feeding 172 exterior fans per building, with zero evaporative water consumption. NVIDIA's DSX architecture pushes the same idea further — a sealed 75% propylene-glycol loop at return temperatures up to 45°C, supporting racks above 100 kW. Meta's greenfield campuses have moved to sealed dry-cooling coils with WUE near 0.20 L/kWh, dropping to 0.0 in cooler hours.[3]

The trade-off is real: in warm climates, dry coolers need more fan power, which nudges PUE up. Operators compensate by raising server inlet temperatures above 27°C, expanding the hours of passive economization. The power problem

Even with efficient cooling, a gigawatt-scale campus needs a gigawatt of reliable power. Solar and wind alone can't deliver 24/7 baseload without enormous storage, and transmission interconnection queues in hubs like Northern Virginia and Texas run 5 to 7 years — while a compute shell erects in 12 to 18 months.[4]

Hyperscalers are responding with "bring-your-own-power": long-term nuclear PPAs and behind-the-meter colocation at plant busbars. Constellation is spending $1.6 billion to recommission Three Mile Island Unit 1 (835 MW) under a 20-year PPA with Microsoft. Google signed a 500 MW fleet agreement with Kairos Power's Generation IV SMRs. Amazon bought the 960 MW Cumulus campus behind Talen's Susquehanna plant.[5]

But behind-the-meter colocation collides with ratepayer law. In November 2024, FERC rejected the amended Susquehanna interconnection agreement, ruling that isolating large loads from transmission tariffs could shift up to $140 million a year onto residential and commercial ratepayers. The full docket analysis is on the baseload page.

The regulatory response

While the U.S. regulates through wholesale market rules, Europe regulates by statute. Germany's EnEfG caps new data centers at PUE ≤ 1.2, mandates 100% renewable procurement, and requires waste-heat export quotas rising from 10% to 20% — effectively treating data centers as municipal heating assets. Ireland's CRU requires 80% renewable additionality and 100% on-site backup. Singapore's DC-CFA2 caps PUE at 1.25 in a tropical climate. The EU's recast Energy Efficiency Directive creates a mandatory reporting database for facilities above 500 kW.[6]

These mandates change site selection: proximity to district heating networks is now a legal requirement in Germany, not a nice-to-have. The comparative matrix is on the regulation page.
