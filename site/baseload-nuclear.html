Grid Interconnection & Baseload

Clean baseload: commercial nuclear restarts, SMRs, and behind-the-meter colocation

Multi-gigawatt transmission queues create 5- to 7-year delays in major compute hubs. Hyperscale developers are responding by securing baseload commercial nuclear PPAs and colocating directly behind generator busbars — and colliding with ratepayer law.

GreenCompute Research · Updated September 3, 2026

The 5-to-7 year interconnection disparity

A modern hyperscale AI shell can be erected in 12 to 18 months. But connecting a multi-hundred-megawatt or gigawatt facility to the high-voltage transmission grid takes 5 to 7 years in regional transmission organizations such as PJM (serving Northern Virginia's 12 GW market) and ERCOT in Texas.[1]

To bypass these bottlenecks, hyperscalers are turning to "Bring-Your-Own-Power" (BYOP) behind-the-meter colocation — placing facilities directly at power plant substations. Roughly 30% of planned U.S. data center capacity now incorporates behind-the-meter generation, with about 90% of those plans announced in recent development cycles.[1]

The Crane Clean Energy Center (Three Mile Island Unit 1)

In September 2024, Constellation Energy finalized a 20-year Power Purchase Agreement with Microsoft to purchase 100% of the generating output (835 MW) of Three Mile Island Unit 1. Unit 1 operated safely until its economic shutdown in 2019 and is physically separate from Unit 2, which ceased operation in 1979 following a partial meltdown and remains in active decommissioning.[2]

The $1.6 billion restoration — supported by a $1 billion Department of Energy loan guarantee — funds complete overhauls of main power transformers, steam generators, turbines, and control systems, targeting commercial operation between 2027 and 2028. To prevent PJM transmission studies from delaying delivery until 2031, Constellation petitioned FERC to transfer interconnection capacity rights from its retired fossil-fueled Eddystone generating station to the Crane facility.[2]

Google and Kairos Power: the first Gen-IV SMR fleet

In October 2024, Google executed a corporate agreement to deploy 500 MW of zero-carbon power across a fleet of Generation IV Small Modular Reactors from Kairos Power by 2035. The initial deployment centers on the Hermes 2 fluoride-salt-cooled high-temperature reactor (50 MW) in Oak Ridge, Tennessee, scheduled to supply the TVA grid to support Google facilities by 2030.[3]

The Kairos deal is the first U.S. Gen-IV SMR corporate PPA — a test of whether advanced reactors can deliver the 24/7 baseload that renewable-only campuses cannot.[3]

Regulatory conflict: FERC Docket ER24-2172 (AWS / Susquehanna)

Amazon Web Services purchased the 960 MW Cumulus data center campus in Luzerne County, Pennsylvania, for $650 million, connecting directly behind-the-meter to Talen Energy's 2,520 MW Susquehanna nuclear power station. The long-term agreement structures power delivery in 120 MW increments up to 960 MW, with PPA expansions totaling 1,920 MW.[1]

In November 2024, FERC issued a landmark ruling in Docket ER24-2172, rejecting an amended Interconnection Service Agreement that would have expanded Amazon's behind-the-meter load from 300 MW to 480 MW without standard transmission charges. Intervening transmission utilities (American Electric Power and Exelon) argued the facility would inevitably rely on the regional grid for operational backup, frequency stability, and voltage support — shifting up to $140 million annually in network transmission costs onto retail ratepayers. In a 2-1 decision, FERC ruled that PJM failed to justify deviations from standard pro forma interconnection agreements.[1]

The ruling establishes that behind-the-meter colocation faces rigorous federal oversight on cost socialization — the U.S. regulatory counterweight to the statutory mandates on the regulation page.[1]
